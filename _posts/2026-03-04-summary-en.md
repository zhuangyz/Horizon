Relocking the bootloader after installation is crucial to re-enable security features like verified boot, which checks the integrity of the OS at startup.

The findings, published in Nature Communications on February 23, reveal interface roughness defects formed during the optimized growth process of semiconductor structures. This breakthrough is significant because these tiny, atomic-scale defects can sabotage the performance and reliability of nearly all modern electronics, from smartphones and AI data centers to quantum computers. The ability to directly image and understand these flaws during the development phase could revolutionize chip debugging, fault isolation, and ultimately lead to more robust and efficient semiconductor manufacturing processes. The technique specifically images and measures the roughness at the interfaces between different materials within a chip, a critical parameter previously difficult to quantify at the atomic scale. This collaboration between academia (Cornell) and industry leaders (TSMC, ASM) indicates the method's direct relevance to cutting-edge semiconductor fabrication.

'Mouse bite' is a colloquial term researchers use to describe tiny, irregular atomic-scale imperfections at material interfaces. High-resolution 3D imaging techniques, like advanced electron microscopy, are essential tools for metrology (measurement science) in semiconductor manufacturing to inspect and characterize these increasingly small features.
